Teaching a Small LLM to Read Time Series: From ChatTS to LFM3-TS

A Visual Guide to Patch Encoders, Reading Losses, a Three-Stage Training Recipe, the ChatTS Benchmark, and Anomaly Detection on Real Series

November 2026 18 min read Nardi Xhepi

Language models are good with words and, let's be honest, not great with numbers. Paste a few hundred sensor readings into a chat window and ask where the anomaly is, and you will usually get a confident, fluent, and wrong answer.

I wanted to find out how far a small model can get on this problem if it reads the series directly instead of reading digits. The result is LFM3-TS: LFM3-1.7B from Liquid AI with a time-series encoder in front, built on the ideas and the synthetic data of the ChatTS paper, with a training recipe of my own. You give it a series and a question, and it tells you if there is an anomaly, where it is, and what it looks like, as shown in the figure below.

You might expect a 1.8B model to land well below ChatTS-8B, which is more than four times larger. On the benchmark of the ChatTS paper, it does: LFM3-TS is 2 to 6 points behind. On real series from sources that neither model was trained on, it is the other way around. LFM3-TS finds more of the anomalies (64% versus 53%), and it raises fewer than half as many false alarms (28 versus 61).

So, how does a language model read a series, what does the training recipe look like, and how does the result compare with ChatTS-8B? I aim to answer all of these in this article. The results come in two parts: first the benchmark of the ChatTS paper, since that is the work this model is built on, and then anomaly detection on real series.

PS: I am not affiliated with Liquid AI or with the ChatTS authors, and this is a personal project trained on rented Colab GPUs. Please don't read the comparison as a verdict on ChatTS, which is the work that made this one possible.

1. Why can't a language model just read the numbers?

A language model reads tokens, and a time series is a list of numbers. The obvious trick is to write the numbers as text. It works, in the sense that the model produces an answer, but it has two problems.

First, it is expensive, because each number breaks into several tokens. Second, and this is the bigger issue, the model loses track of where it is: to say "the spike is at point 301", it would have to count commas. The figure below shows the alternative.

ChatTS applied this idea to time series, and LFM3-TS does the same on a much smaller language model (with a different training recipe; more on that in sections 3 and 4).

2. The LFM3-TS architecture

The design is simple: a time-series encoder and a projector sit in front of the language model. The architecture of the language model is untouched, which means the weights of LFM3-1.7B load without changes (training then updates them; more on that in section 2.2). 2.1 Four design decisions

Most of the model is standard. Four decisions do the real work, as illustrated below.

2.2 What is trained

The model has 1,781 million parameters, and training updates 1,519 million of them: the new encoder and projector at a learning rate of 2e-4, and the whole language model at a much gentler 1e-5. Only the token embeddings (262 million) stay frozen.

3. A model can learn to talk without reading

The obvious way to train such a model is on the ChatTS training data alone. There is a trap in it. The loss goes down nicely, the answers look great, and the numbers in them can still be wrong: the model learns to write like a model that reads time series.

The cause is in the data, as shown in the figure below.

Two things fixed it, and the figure below shows both.

3.1 Questions with one answer per token

I wrote a generator of questions on synthetic series, where the answer can come from the series and from nowhere else. Three types of question give one target for each series token (the left panel above).

Other questions are about a single event: a spike, a level shift, a trend, a period, or the value at a given point. In all of these answers, every number is a fact the model has to read. There is no phrasing to hide behind.

A second family of questions asks for values in the unit of the series itself: the value at a given point, the mean of a block, and the lowest and highest value of a stretch. (The encoder only sees a standardized series, so to give a real value the model has to combine what the tokens hold with the scale written in the prompt; see panel (c) of Figure 4.)

3.2 A second loss on the series tokens

The second fix (the right panel above) is a small linear layer that rebuilds the 8 points of a patch from its token. Its error is added to the training loss. The layer is not part of the released model.

One caveat, to be fair: I have not measured how much each of the two fixes contributes on its own.

4. The three-stage training recipe

The recipe has three stages, and each one starts from the weights of the one before, as illustrated below. All stages train the full language model, the encoder, and the projector, and everything fits on one GPU with 95 GB of memory on Google Colab.

4.1 The stages

  1. Learn to read. The model sees generated questions only: positions, block means, and values in the unit of the series. At the end of this stage, it gives the exact point of a spike.
  2. Read long series. The same questions, on series of up to 2,048 points. This stage looks optional. It isn't: without it, the positions go wrong after point 512.
  3. Learn to answer. The model sees a mixture of five data types, and each type has one job (see the table below).
Data typeShareJob
ChatTS synthetic data48%Teaches the model to describe a series and to answer questions about it.
Generated questions19%Keeps the exact reading alive, and teaches the answer "this series is normal".
Plain chat15%Keeps the model a chat model. The answers come from the original LFM3-1.7B.
Real series, labelled10%Shows the model real anomalies with labels (section 4.2).
Real series with events put in8%Teaches that an anomaly is what a series does not usually do (section 4.3).

4.2 Adding real series

Synthetic data doesn't contain all the shapes of real signals, so I added windows from TSB-AD-U, a public benchmark of labelled series from web services, servers, ECG recordings, and motion sensors. The windows have 256 to 1,024 points.

Now, these labels have a catch. They say where the anomaly is, but not what it looks like, and a chat model needs something to say. The figure below shows how each labelled window becomes a training answer.

The training file has 1,900 windows. Approximately half of them have no anomaly, and the correct answer is then simply "No".

4.3 An anomaly is what the series does not usually do

So far, so good. But labelled windows have a weakness as training data. There are few of them, and a model can take a shortcut: "a spike is an anomaly", full stop. In many real series, spikes are the normal behaviour, and a model that learned the shortcut cries wolf on every one of them.

So, how do we teach a model what "unusual" means for a given series? The last 8% of the mixture is built for exactly that. I take real windows without a labelled anomaly and put an event into them: a spike, a short drop, a shift of the level, or a stretch of larger swings, sized against what that series already does. Each window then becomes one of four cases, as shown below.

Two details are worth mentioning. First, the script that puts the event in knows its exact place and values, so every number in these training answers is true by construction. Second, a third of these examples ask in two turns: "what are the notable events in this series?", then "is any of them an anomaly?". (That way, describing and judging are two separate answers.)

5. Part one: the ChatTS benchmark

Now that we have covered how the model is built and trained, how good is it? Since LFM3-TS is built on ChatTS, I thought it made the most sense to start with the benchmark of the ChatTS paper itself. It has two sets: set A, with 159 records on real series, and set B, with 400 records on synthetic series. Each record holds one or several series and asks several numbered questions about them: the trend, the seasonality, the noise, the local changes, and, when there are several series, which of them behave alike.

I ran both models on both sets and scored the answers with the scoring script of the ChatTS repository, unchanged. It gives two scores. The categorical score checks the kind of thing the answer names (an upward trend, a downward spike). The numerical score checks how close a position, an amplitude, or a period is. (A third score, for reasoning questions, needs a judge language model for most of its questions, and I left it out.)

Set A, categoricalSet A, numericalSet B, categoricalSet B, numerical
LFM3-TS (1.8B)82.0%77.5%79.5%76.8%
ChatTS-8B83.8%82.3%84.6%82.9%

Set A: 299 categorical and 273 numerical questions. Set B: 1,069 categorical and 708 numerical questions.

ChatTS-8B is ahead on all four scores: by 2 to 5 points on set A, and by 5 to 6 points on set B. LFM3-TS has less than a quarter of the parameters, so some gap is expected. Where does it come from? The figure below splits set B, the larger of the two, by task.

Three things stand out:

  • The two models are level on trend and seasonality. On the categorical score, that's 96% versus 98% for the trend, and 100% for both on seasonality.
  • LFM3-TS is behind on local changes. It names the right kind of local change in 79% of the questions, versus 88% for ChatTS-8B.
  • The largest gap is on comparing several series by shape. Asked which series of a group have the same shape, LFM3-TS scores 73% and ChatTS-8B 88%.

Both sets ask about properties that the question names: "what is the trend?", "is there a spike?". The second part of the results asks a more open question, on series from the wild.

6. Part two: anomalies in real series

This is the part I was most curious about. A benchmark question tells the model what to look for; a real series doesn't. (As a side note, this is also where the two models differ the most.)

6.1 The test set

The test set has 350 windows of 512 points from TSB-AD-U, none of which the model saw during training. A "source" is one dataset of the benchmark, for example the Yahoo dataset. The test set has two parts:

Part of the test setWindows with an anomalyWindows without
New sources105129
Known sources, new recordings4967

Both models get the same question: is there an anomaly, and where? For a window without an anomaly, the correct answer is "no". (How much does the 16-point margin matter? Quite a bit, for one of the two models; more on that in section 6.3.)

Precision: of the windows where the model reports an anomaly, the share where the place is correct. Recall: of the windows with an anomaly, the share where the model gives the correct place. False alarms: the number of windows without an anomaly where the model reports one.

Looking at the new sources, I take away four things:

  • LFM3-TS finds more of the anomalies. It puts 64% of them at the correct place, and ChatTS-8B 53%. For clear anomalies, the two are close (88% and 83%); the difference comes from the subtle ones (48% and 34%).
  • LFM3-TS raises fewer than half as many false alarms. It reports an anomaly in 22% of the windows that have none. ChatTS-8B does so in 47% of them.
  • A "yes" from LFM3-TS means more. When LFM3-TS reports an anomaly, the place is correct in 66% of the windows, versus 40% for ChatTS-8B.
  • LFM3-TS doesn't just memorize its sources. It finds 73% of the anomalies of known sources and 64% of those of new sources. That's a drop, as you'd expect, but not a collapse, and its false-alarm rate is the same on both.

(One group I would not read anything into: the subtle anomalies from known sources. It has only 10 windows, so a single window moves the result by 10 points.)

6.3 How exact is the place?

The 16-point margin is generous, so I also scored the place without it. LFM3-TS answers with a stretch ("around points 255 to 257"), and ChatTS-8B, which I asked for a point, answers with one point. In the table below, "overlap" means that the answer touches the labelled stretch, and "within 16" is the rule used everywhere else in this article.

LFM3-TS barely moves: 60% of the anomalies with no margin at all, versus 64% with 16 points. ChatTS-8B needs the margin: its recall goes from 29% to 53%. In other words, LFM3-TS points at the anomaly and ChatTS-8B points near it. (To be fair to ChatTS-8B, a single point is a harder target than a stretch.)

How well do the stretches of LFM3-TS match the labels? On the 73 anomalies of new sources where it says "yes", its stretch covers 63% of the labelled points on average, and the overlap between the two (intersection over union) is 0.39. So it finds the right place, but not the exact extent.

6.5 Can it read a value?

Finding the place is one thing. But a chat model also quotes values, and a wrong value in a confident sentence is worse than no value at all. So I tested that separately, with 600 questions: half on generated series and half on the real test windows. The questions are "what is the value at point N?" and "what are the lowest and the highest value between points A and B?". A value counts as correct if it is within a quarter of a standard deviation of the series.

Value at a pointMedian errorLowest value of a stretchHighest value of a stretch
Real series57%0.18 sd64%56%
Generated series65%0.16 sd75%81%

LFM3-TS only. Percentages: share of the answers within a quarter of a standard deviation (sd) of the series. Median error: for the value at a point, in standard deviations.

On real series, LFM3-TS gives the right value a bit more than half of the time, with a median error of 0.18 standard deviations. That's good enough to say "around 1,250", and not good enough to trust the last digit.

6.6 How true are the descriptions?

When LFM3-TS answers "yes", it also describes what happens, and a description is made of claims that can be checked: a value at a point, a level before, a kind of change. So I had a language model (Claude Sonnet 5.5) check them. For each of the 154 "yes" answers on the test windows, it split the description into claims and compared every claim with the values of the series, which it read through a small script. It did not know where the labelled anomaly was. For a value, the tolerance is the same quarter of a standard deviation as above.

The result: 72% of the 511 claims are true, and 44% of the answers contain no false claim at all. The table below splits the claims by kind.

Kind of claimClaimsTrue
The kind of change (a spike, a drop, a shift)9581%
A level before or after15377%
A value at a named point17071%
A duration or a return4764%
A comparison with the rest of the series3152%

Is the training data to blame? I ran the same check on a sample of 96 answers of the training file, and 94% of their claims are true. So the training answers are sound, and the gap is in what the model makes of them. It is best at naming the kind of change and weakest when it compares a stretch with the rest of the series. My advice: read a description as a pointer to where to look, and check the numbers it quotes.

6.7 Side note: is it still a chat model?

Training every weight of a language model on series tasks can easily wreck what it could do before, which is why 15% of the stage 3 mixture is plain chat. To check that this worked, I gave LFM3-TS and the original LFM3-1.7B the same 200 arithmetic problems from GSM8K. The original solves 77.5% of them and LFM3-TS 71.0%. With 200 problems, the noise is about ±6 points, so a gap of 6.5 points is at the edge of it. I'd summarize it as "a small loss", not as "no loss".

7. Availability

The weights, the code, and the full training recipe are public:

  • Model: huggingface.co/nardi/LFM3-TS
  • Code and training scripts: github.com/nardi-xhepi/lfm3-ts

The code follows the conventions of the transformers library, so the model loads with the usual Auto classes (you need transformers 5.23 or later, the first release with LFM3 built in).

Conclusion

All in all, there is nothing fundamentally new in LFM3-TS. The architecture is the usual encoder-plus-language-model design, and the core idea and half of the training data come from ChatTS. What I added is the part that forces the model to read, and the part that teaches it what "unusual" means for a given series.

The outcome is a trade. On the ChatTS benchmark, the 1.8B model stays 2 to 6 points behind the 8B one. On real series it has never seen, it finds more anomalies and is much more careful about crying wolf. Its descriptions are the part I'd work on next: about seven claims in ten are true, and the training data is not what holds it back.

Sure, I'd guess that a dedicated detector with a tuned threshold still does better on subtle anomalies (I did not test this, so take it as an educated guess). But a detector doesn't tell you what it saw, and it doesn't answer a follow-up question.

If there is one thing I'd take away from this project, it's that a falling loss says very little about whether a multimodal model uses its new modality. Check it directly, for example with questions that can't be answered any other way.
